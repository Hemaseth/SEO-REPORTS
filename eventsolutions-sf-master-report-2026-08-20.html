Executive Summary

The one-page picture

For a 90-second read before the meeting.

The launch did not damage your content — and that is the important part

  • Zero pages lost. All 74 pre-launch URLs still resolve with a 200. No 404s, no 410s, no redirect chains — every redirect is a single hop.
  • Zero pages lost body copy. The 62 pages present on both sides of the cutover were re-measured with one parser applied identically to old and new HTML. Not one page shrank. The homepage went 577 → 777 words, the corporate page 824 → 1,107.
  • The four empty template shells found on 25 July are fixed. No page on the site now returns 200 with nothing in it.
  • Canonical discipline is complete and correct. All 182 pages self-canonical, all on the www host, all index, follow. No staging noindex survived the cutover.
  • Speed is a non-issue. 130–140 ms time-to-first-byte on money pages from Vercel's edge. This should not absorb any remediation budget.
  • Image accessibility is complete. 4,046 images, zero missing alt attributes. The 97 empty-alt cases are decorative and correctly marked.
  • Your llms.txt is genuinely good. 16 KB, correctly structured, with a categorised page index carrying real descriptions. Most competitors in this category publish nothing here.

What actually threatens the rankings you are trying to hold

  • The structured-data layer was replaced, not ported. BreadcrumbList went from 74 pages to 0. SearchAction from 74 to 0. WebSite from 74 to 2. Organization and ImageObject from 74 to about half. These are precisely the types answer engines and rich results depend on.
  • The previous WordPress site is still published. It now blocks Googlebot — but explicitly allows every other crawler, including GPTBot, PerplexityBot and ClaudeBot. A self-canonical full duplicate of your content remains freely harvestable by exactly the systems this engagement is meant to protect. Raised on 6 August; still open.
  • Two-thirds of your sitemap is furniture. 125 of 182 URLs are tag, category and author archives — up from 36 before the launch. Each now carries about 400 words of chrome around a handful of links. Only 57 URLs are pages a customer or an answer engine has any reason to read.
  • Your six revenue pages are half-duplicates of each other. After stripping every phrase that appears on more than half the site, they remain 46–56% mutually identical. A holiday-party page and a City Hall wedding page share more than half their distinctive language.
  • Measurement lapsed at cutover. Google Analytics survived. Google Tag Manager did not, and neither did the Search Console verification tag — so conversion tracking is likely incomplete and the ability to verify any of this may have lapsed with it.
  • Your best ranking could not be reproduced today. /corporate-holiday-party-ideas/ ranked #1 on 3 August. It did not appear in today's pull. The page is alive, indexed and unchanged — but this was measured with a different instrument, so it needs a first-party check before it is treated as a loss.
The shape of the problem. Everything a person can see survived the launch. Everything a machine reads did not. That is an unusual failure mode and a favourable one — restoring machine-readable signals is template work at the layout level, not a rewrite of the site.

Six findings that put current visibility at risk

Critical 1

The previous WordPress site is still published, and every AI crawler can still read it

https://lightskyblue-porcupine-787632.hostingersite.com/ returns HTTP 200, 95,595 bytes, full page set intact, six weeks after cutover. Its sitemap_index.xml also returns 200 and actively offers the full URL set.

Something did change since this was raised on 6 August. The host now serves a robots.txt that disallows Googlebot — and explicitly allows everything else.

That is a partial fix that leaves the half this engagement is about untouched. Two reasons:

  • Blocking crawl does not remove anything from an index. A URL Google has already indexed and can no longer fetch stays in the index — often for a long time, precisely because Google can no longer read the page to learn it should be dropped. The page still declares index, follow and still canonicalises to itself, not to your domain — and Googlebot can no longer read either of those statements.
  • Every non-Google crawler retains full access. That includes Bingbot, and it includes GPTBot, PerplexityBot, ClaudeBot, CCBot, Amazonbot and Applebot-Extended. For answer engines, nothing was fixed. A self-canonical full duplicate of your content, with the same title and the same positioning, remains freely harvestable on a domain you do not control and cannot update.
Direction — crawl-blocking is the wrong instrument for the outcome required here. What is needed is a disposition decision that removes the legacy property as an independent source for all agents, not one that hides it from a single crawler. The available mechanisms differ sharply in speed, in whether residual equity is preserved, and in whether they affect non-Google agents at all. Selecting among them, and sequencing that against the apex-to-www consolidation already in flight, is part of the engagement scope.

Why each one costs you. BreadcrumbList is what produces the site-hierarchy path instead of a raw URL in a search result, and it is one of the few explicit statements a page makes about where it sits in a site — three of the four competitors benchmarked on 3 August carry it, so the site went from parity to absent. SearchAction forecloses the sitelinks search box. WebSite is the anchor node of a site's entity graph: 180 pages now assert a business without asserting the site that publishes them, which is exactly the join an answer engine needs when assembling a citation.

The entity data itself is good. It is the graph wiring around it that thinned.

Direction — restoring the five lost types is template work at the layout level rather than per-page work, and it returns the site to its own pre-launch parity — a restoration, not an enhancement. The @id graph wiring, node placement and validation pass are part of the engagement scope.

Critical 6

The site's best ranking could not be reproduced today — and needs first-party verification before it is called a loss

On 3 August, /corporate-holiday-party-ideas/ ranked #1 for corporate holiday party ideas San Francisco. In today's pull the domain does not appear anywhere in that result set; the results are venue pages and listicles.

What is confirmed: the page is alive, unchanged and indexed. It returns 200, sits in the sitemap, carries index, follow, grew from 2,279 to 2,680 words at the launch, and a targeted search surfaces it directly.

What is not confirmed: that the position was actually lost. The 3 August baseline came from a Google SERP scraper returning ranked organic positions; that tool's quota is exhausted, so today's reading came from a different instrument whose ordinals are not interchangeable with Google positions. Two instruments, one query, six weeks apart, on a seasonal term whose competitive set reshapes as autumn approaches. The honest statement is that the site's single best ranking could not be reproduced and the reason is not yet known.

Two contributing factors are measured and hold regardless of which instrument is right:

  • The page receives 8 internal links, against 181 for every page in the navigation. The site's strongest asset is one of its least internally supported.
  • It lost BreadcrumbList and Organization markup at the launch along with every other page.
Direction — settle this against Search Console impressions and average position for that query before treating it as a ranking loss or spending remediation effort on it. Search Console is authoritative where an external pull is not. If the loss is real, the internal-linking and markup factors above are where the recovery work starts — scoping that is part of the engagement scope.

Methodology

How this was measured

Every figure in this report traces to a measurement taken on 20 August 2026. Nothing is estimated.

  • Fresh crawl. All 182 sitemap URLs fetched on 20 August and cached to disk. Every subsequent measurement reads that cache — nothing was re-fetched or inferred.
  • Launch acceptance diff. The 74 pre-launch URLs from the 14 July migration baseline were each followed live to record status, redirect count and final destination. The 74 raw pre-cutover HTML files were re-parsed with the same extractor used on the live crawl, so old and new word counts are directly comparable — the standard trap in migration audits, and the reason this report can state that no content was lost.
  • Structured data. JSON-LD parsed from served HTML on both sides, with @type collected recursively through nested nodes. Identical detection on both sides, so the diff holds regardless of parser quirks.
  • Content uniqueness. Five-word phrase shingles across all 56 pages and posts; phrases appearing on more than half the site removed as boilerplate; pairwise similarity computed on what remained.
  • Link graph. Every anchor in every cached page resolved to an absolute URL and classified. In-body counts exclude navigation, header and footer.
  • Live search. Same-day pulls for the query set used in the 3 August benchmark, plus brand-scoped queries.
  • Supporting documents. Eight companion documents hold the full per-URL evidence behind every section of this report.
